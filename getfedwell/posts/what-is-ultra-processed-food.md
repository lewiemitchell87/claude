# What Is Ultra-Processed Food? A Plain-English Guide for Families

If you've been hearing the term "ultra-processed food" everywhere lately and
quietly wondering what it actually *means* — you're not alone. It's on the news,
in the headlines, all over social media. But the definitions are often confusing,
a bit preachy, or so strict they make you feel like you've failed before you've
started.

So let's keep this simple. Here's what ultra-processed food really is, how to spot
it, why it's worth paying attention to — and, crucially, how to eat less of it
without turning your kitchen (or your life) upside down.

This is the page we wish someone had handed us when we started. No guilt, no
food-policing. Just the facts and a sensible plan.

## The quick version

**Ultra-processed food (UPF)** is food that has been put through industrial
processes and built from ingredients you'd never find in a normal home kitchen —
things like emulsifiers, stabilisers, modified starches, protein isolates,
artificial colours, sweeteners and flavourings.

A handy rule of thumb: **if it contains an ingredient you wouldn't recognise as
food, or couldn't reasonably buy and cook with at home, it's probably
ultra-processed.**

That's not the whole story, but it'll get you 80% of the way there in the
supermarket aisle.

## Where the term comes from: the NOVA groups

The "ultra-processed" label comes from a system called **NOVA**, developed by
researchers led by Professor Carlos Monteiro in Brazil. Instead of classifying
food by nutrients (fat, sugar, salt), NOVA classifies it by **how much and what
kind of processing** it's been through. There are four groups:

**Group 1 — Unprocessed or minimally processed foods**
Foods in (more or less) their natural state. Fresh, frozen or dried fruit and
veg, plain meat and fish, eggs, milk, plain yoghurt, dried beans and lentils,
rice, oats, nuts. Things may have been cleaned, frozen or dried — but nothing's
been added.

**Group 2 — Processed culinary ingredients**
The things you cook *with*: oils, butter, salt, sugar, honey, vinegar. You don't
eat these on their own; you use them to turn Group 1 foods into meals.

**Group 3 — Processed foods**
Group 1 foods with Group 2 ingredients added, usually to preserve them or make
them tastier: tinned tomatoes or beans, tinned fish, cheese, salted nuts, and
simple freshly-made bread. Still made from recognisable ingredients.

**Group 4 — Ultra-processed foods (UPF)**
Industrial formulations, typically made mostly from cheap refined substances and
additives, with little or no whole food left in them. This is where most packaged
snacks, fizzy drinks, mass-produced bread, ready meals, breakfast cereals,
flavoured yoghurts, chicken nuggets, instant noodles and "diet" shakes live.

It's that **fourth group** everyone's talking about.

## How to spot UPF in real life

You don't need to memorise the science. Here are three quick checks for the
supermarket:

1. **Flip it over and read the ingredients.** A long list with names you don't
   recognise — *emulsifiers, maltodextrin, modified maize starch, glucose-fructose
   syrup, flavourings, colours, sweeteners* — is the clearest sign of UPF.
2. **Ask: "Could I make a home version of this with kitchen ingredients?"**
   Plain yoghurt? Yes. A strawberry "yoghurt" with thickeners, flavourings and
   sweeteners? No — that's ultra-processed.
3. **Watch for "health-halo" packaging.** Plenty of products marketed as healthy
   — protein bars, cereal bars, low-fat flavoured yoghurts, plant-based "meats",
   meal-replacement shakes — are ultra-processed. Front-of-pack claims tell you
   nothing; the ingredients list does.

## Why does it matter?

A fair question — humans have processed food for thousands of years, and not all
processing is bad (freezing, tinning and fermenting are all brilliant).

The concern is specifically with **ultra-processed** food, and here's where the
evidence stands, told straight:

- **A lot of large studies** have found that people who eat more UPF tend to have
  higher rates of obesity, type 2 diabetes and heart disease. These are
  *associations* — they show a link, not proof that UPF alone causes each problem.
- **One carefully controlled trial** (run by Kevin Hall at the US National
  Institutes of Health in 2019) is especially striking: when people were fed an
  ultra-processed diet, they ate around **500 more calories a day** and gained
  weight, compared with the same people on a minimally-processed diet matched for
  fat, sugar, salt and fibre. The food was *designed* to be easy to overeat.
- UPF tends to be **energy-dense, soft, and engineered to be moreish** — easy to
  eat quickly and easy to eat too much of.

In the UK this matters because UPF makes up **more than half** of the average
adult's calories — and an even higher share for children and teenagers. It's not a
fringe issue; for most British families it's the default.

> **An honest note:** the science is still developing, and we're not here to
> scare you. You don't need to fear a single packet of biscuits. The goal is to
> shift the *balance* of what your family eats over time — not to achieve some
> impossible 100%.

## So… do we have to be perfect? (No.)

This is the bit that trips people up. The all-or-nothing version of non-UPF eating
is expensive, exhausting and, frankly, miserable — which is exactly why people give
up on it.

That's not what Get Fed Well is about. Our whole approach is **better, not
perfect:**

- Swap the few UPFs you eat *most often* first — that's where the biggest wins are.
- Cook a bit more from scratch when you realistically can, and don't beat yourself
  up when you can't.
- Keep the convenience foods that genuinely make your life work, and chip away at
  the rest.

Cutting from 80% UPF to 40% UPF is a huge, meaningful change — and it's achievable
for a normal family with jobs, kids and a budget. Perfection isn't the target.
Progress is.

## Easy first swaps to get you started

Want a quick win today? Try one of these:

- **Flavoured yoghurt → plain yoghurt + fresh or frozen fruit** (and a drizzle of
  honey if you like).
- **Shop-bought breakfast cereal → porridge oats** with fruit and a little honey.
- **Squash or fizzy drinks → water, sparkling water, or fruit slices in water.**
- **Mass-produced sliced bread → a simple bakery or "real bread" loaf** (we'll cover
  exactly which supermarket loaves pass in a future post).
- **Shop-bought sauces → a quick homemade tomato sauce** from a tin of tomatoes,
  garlic and herbs. Ten minutes, and it freezes brilliantly.

You don't have to do all of these. Pick **one** this week. That's how this works.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is all processed food bad for you?**
No. Tinned beans, frozen veg, plain yoghurt and simple cheese are processed and
perfectly good foods. The concern is specifically with *ultra*-processed
foods (NOVA Group 4) — industrial formulations full of additives and refined
ingredients.

**Is bread ultra-processed?**
It depends entirely on the bread. A simple loaf of flour, water, yeast and salt
is not ultra-processed. Most long-life supermarket sliced bread, with emulsifiers
and preservatives, is. (We've got a whole post coming on how to tell them apart.)

**Is eating non-UPF more expensive?**
It can be if you shop in health-food aisles — but it doesn't have to be. Basics
like oats, eggs, tinned beans, frozen veg, rice and whole chickens are some of the
cheapest food in the shop. We'll show you a real budget weekly shop soon.

**Do I have to cut out UPF completely?**
Absolutely not. Aim to shift the balance of your family's diet over time. Cutting
down meaningfully is a real win — it doesn't have to be all or nothing.
